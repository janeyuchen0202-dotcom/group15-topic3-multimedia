Frequency-Domain Transformations

A frequency transform rewrites the same pixels or samples as weights of simple patterns. This page compares the DFT, DCT and DWT and shows why compression and filtering are easier in that form.

Overview

Same data, different coordinates. In the spatial (or time) domain an image is a grid of brightness values. A frequency transform rewrites the same N numbers as weights of N basis patterns, from slowly to rapidly varying. Nothing is lost: the inverse transform returns the original exactly. The benefit is that in natural images and sounds most of the energy collects in a few low-frequency weights, and many operations become simpler.

DFT: global frequency content. The discrete Fourier transform uses complex sinusoids. Each coefficient has a magnitude, how much of that frequency is present, and a phase, where the pattern sits. Phase matters more than it looks: many important features of a signal survive when only the phase is kept (Oppenheim & Lim, 1981). The fast Fourier transform needs about N log N operations instead of N² (Cooley & Tukey, 1965), and because convolution becomes multiplication in the frequency domain, large filters become cheap. The DFT treats the signal as periodic, so a jump between opposite edges spreads energy across many coefficients.

DCT: real cosines for blocks. The discrete cosine transform (Ahmed, Natarajan & Rao, 1974) uses only real cosines. Its basis matches mirror-symmetric boundary conditions (Strang, 1999), so a block has no artificial jump at its edges, and its performance comes close to that of the optimal Karhunen–Loève transform. Baseline JPEG therefore transforms 8 × 8 blocks with the DCT and then quantises the coefficients (Wallace, 1992).

DWT: frequency with location. Wavelets are short waves that are shifted and scaled. Mallat’s (1989) pyramid algorithm filters an image into an approximation and detail subbands, named LL, HL, LH and HH in JPEG 2000, and repeats on the approximation. The result is a multiresolution representation that lies between the spatial and Fourier domains. JPEG 2000 uses a 9/7 filter for lossy and a 5/3 filter for lossless coding (Skodras et al., 2001).

Using the frequency view. Removing high frequencies blurs an image, and removing low frequencies leaves its edges, as the OpenCV Fourier tutorial demonstrates. Compression discards small coefficients, and analysis tools read spectra to find periodic noise or textures. The sampling rate from Digital Multimedia Representation decides which frequencies exist at all, and Efficient Multimedia Processing explains when the FFT beats direct filtering.

Applications

JPEG photographs

JPEG transforms 8 × 8 blocks with the DCT, quantises the coefficients into small integers and entropy-codes them. The quality setting mainly controls the quantization step size. The DCT-based Baseline method is by far the most widely implemented JPEG method.

Sources: Wallace (1992); Szeliski (2011), §2.3.3; ITU-T T.81.

Fingerprints and JPEG 2000

The FBI’s WSQ standard compresses 8-bit grayscale fingerprints scanned at 500 dpi with a 64-subband wavelet decomposition, scalar quantization and Huffman coding. JPEG 2000 applies its DWT to tiles of the image.

Sources: Bradley, Brislawn & Hopper (1993); Skodras et al. (2001).

Fast filtering and alignment

SciPy’s fftconvolve is generally much faster than direct convolution for large arrays (n > ~500), and scipy.signal.convolve picks the faster method automatically. Google’s HDR+ camera pipeline uses FFT-based alignment to line up burst frames.

Sources: SciPy documentation; Hasinoff et al. (2016).

Learning reflection

I used to think of the frequency domain as a different kind of data. Working through the 8 × 8 example changed that: the 64 coefficients are the same 64 pixels in a new basis, and the inverse DCT gives them back exactly. Information is lost only when we quantise. Comparing the three transforms side by side showed me that the choice depends on what you want to know: which frequencies (DFT), which frequencies in each block (DCT), or which frequencies where (DWT). The phase-only results in Oppenheim and Lim surprised me most. I still find the filter-bank maths of wavelets difficult, and I want to try PyWavelets on a real photo.

Key learning points

  • A transform changes the basis, not the information; quantization is where loss happens.
  • The FFT makes frequency-domain filtering practical by cutting the cost from about N² to N log N.
  • The DCT suits blocks (no edge jump, strong energy compaction); the DWT adds location and several scales.

Misconception

“The magnitude spectrum holds the important information, so phase can be ignored.” Oppenheim and Lim (1981) show that many important features of a signal are preserved when only the phase is kept.

Verified claim

“The default irreversible transform is implemented by means of the Daubechies 9-tap/7-tap filter … The default reversible transformation is implemented by means of the Le Gall 5-tap/3-tap filter.”

Quoted from Skodras, Christopoulos & Ebrahimi (2001), IEEE Signal Processing Magazine 18(5), section “Wavelet Transform”, p. 42.
