Color-Space Transformations

The same colour can be written as different sets of numbers. This page explains RGB, grayscale, HSV and Y′CbCr, how to convert between them, and how chroma subsampling saves data.

Overview

Why convert? A colour space is the coordinate system for the channel values each pixel stores (see Digital Multimedia Representation). Cameras and displays work in RGB, but every RGB channel mixes brightness with colour. Converting reorganises the same information so that the part a task needs sits in its own channel.

RGB. Red, green and blue intensities add up to form colours. Most web images use sRGB, a standard RGB space proposed by Hewlett-Packard and Microsoft in 1996 (Stokes et al.) and later standardised as IEC 61966-2-1. Values written with primes, R′G′B′, are gamma-encoded rather than linear light.

Grayscale. A grayscale image keeps one brightness value per pixel. Luma is a weighted sum, not an average: Y′ = 0.299R′ + 0.587G′ + 0.114B′ in ITU-R BT.601, the weights OpenCV also uses, and 0.2126, 0.7152 and 0.0722 in HDTV’s BT.709. Green counts most and blue least, so a plain average makes greens too dark and blues too bright. Even weighted luma can map clearly different colours to the same grey, which is why better colour-to-gray methods exist (Čadík, 2008).

HSV. Smith (1978) rearranged the RGB cube into hue (an angle), saturation and value. Hue makes colour-based selection easy: OpenCV’s tutorial tracks a blue object by keeping hue values between 110 and 130, because 8-bit OpenCV images store hue divided by 2 (0–179). For greys, where R = G = B, hue is undefined and OpenCV sets it to 0.

Y′CbCr. Video and JPEG apply a linear matrix that separates luma Y′ from two colour-difference signals, Cb (blue minus luma) and Cr (red minus luma). BT.601 studio video places black at code 16 and white at 235, while JPEG’s JFIF format stretches the same signals over the full 0–255 range (ITU-T T.871). Decoding one as the other makes pictures look washed out or too contrasty.

Chroma subsampling. Human vision responds less to fine colour detail than to fine brightness detail, so compression systems keep luma at higher fidelity and store Cb and Cr at lower resolution (Szeliski, 2011, §2.3.3). In 4:2:2 the chroma is halved horizontally, as in BT.601 studio video; in 4:2:0 it is halved in both directions, so an 8-bit pixel costs 12 bits instead of 24. Lossy WebP always uses 4:2:0. JPEG then applies the DCT from Frequency-Domain Transformations to each channel separately.

Applications

JPEG photographs

JPEG files that follow JFIF store Y′CbCr derived from BT.601 but scaled to the full 0–255 range. For still images the chroma is usually averaged both horizontally and vertically before the block transform.

Sources: ITU-T T.871 clause 7; Szeliski (2011), §2.3.3.

WebP on the web

Lossy WebP works only with 8-bit Y′CbCr 4:2:0. Google’s FAQ notes a side effect: converting a PNG with few colours to lossy WebP can give a larger file, because 4:2:0 downsampling introduces new colours that are harder to compress.

Source: Google, WebP FAQ.

Faces in video calls

Chai and Ngan (1999) found faces in videophone images with a skin-colour map applied to the chrominance components, then used the face region to improve the perceptual quality of H.261-coded video.

Source: Chai & Ngan (1999).

Learning reflection

I had always converted images to grayscale by averaging the three channels. Writing this page showed me that luma uses unequal weights, and that even those can merge colours that look clearly different. The Y′CbCr example in Figure 5 made the idea of separating brightness from colour concrete, and the bit counts explained why so much photo and video coding uses chroma subsampling. The most practical lesson was the range difference: JPEG uses 0–255 while studio video uses 16–235, and mixing them up visibly changes contrast. I am still unsure how a video player decides which matrix and range to assume when a file does not say, and I want to look into that next.

Key learning points

  • Converting colour spaces reorganises the same information so that one channel carries what the task needs.
  • Luma is a weighted sum (0.299, 0.587, 0.114 in BT.601), not an average.
  • Y′CbCr makes chroma subsampling possible: 4:2:0 halves the raw data at 8 bits per sample.

Misconception

“Grayscale is the average of R, G and B.” The standards weight green most and blue least (BT.601, BT.709); an equal average darkens greens and brightens blues.

Verified claim

JPEG files that follow JFIF use BT.601-derived Y′CbCr normalised to the full range of 256 levels, whereas BT.601 studio coding puts black at level 16 and peak white at level 235.

Checked in ITU-T T.871 (05/2011), clause 7 “Conversion to and from RGB”, and ITU-R BT.601-7, the quantization levels of the luminance signal.
