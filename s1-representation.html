Digital Multimedia Representation

Every photo, song and video file starts as a list of numbers. This page explains how a continuous signal becomes those numbers, and how four parameters (sampling rate, resolution, bit depth and channels) decide its quality and size.

Overview

Two separate decisions. Light reaching a camera sensor and air pressure reaching a microphone vary continuously. Digitization makes two separate decisions. Sampling chooses where or when to measure: sound is measured thousands of times per second, a picture on a grid of pixels. Quantization chooses how precisely each measurement is written down: every sample is rounded to one of 2b levels, where b is the bit depth.

Sampling rate and resolution. Shannon (1949) showed that a signal containing no frequencies above W hertz is completely determined by samples taken 1/(2W) seconds apart. Sampling more slowly makes high frequencies reappear as false low ones, called aliasing, so converters remove those frequencies before sampling. Images follow the same rule in space: resolution limits the finest detail a picture can hold. A pixel is best understood as a point sample of the scene rather than a little square of colour (A. R. Smith, 1995). That view matters whenever values are rebuilt between samples, as in Image Resizing and Interpolation.

Bit depth. Each extra bit doubles the number of levels and halves the step between them. Rounding adds an error of at most half a step, which behaves like random noise with a standard deviation of about 0.29 steps (S. W. Smith, 1997). Too few levels show up as banding in smooth gradients or as audible noise in quiet passages.

From numbers to media. A grayscale image is a 2-D array with one value per pixel; a colour image stores one value per channel, and what those channels mean is the subject of Color-Space Transformations. Audio is a 1-D sequence per channel, and video is a sequence of images. The raw data rate is samples per second × bits per sample × channels. CD audio needs 44,100 × 16 × 2 ≈ 1.41 Mbit/s and telephone speech 8,000 × 8 = 64 kbit/s, while uncompressed 1080p video at 30 frames per second needs about 1.49 Gbit/s. Numbers this large are why the later sections care about transforming data cleverly and processing it efficiently.

Applications

Digital telephone calls

G.711 samples speech 8,000 times per second and stores each sample in 8 bits, so one call needs 64 kbit/s. The rate is enough because natural-sounding speech needs only about 3.2 kHz of bandwidth. The standard defines two encoding laws, A-law and μ-law, for those 8 bits.

Sources: ITU-T G.711 clauses 2–3; S. W. Smith (1997), Ch. 22.

Compact disc audio

A CD stores 16-bit samples at 44.1 kHz, about 1.4 Mbit/s for stereo. Because the Nyquist limit of 22.05 kHz sits close to the 20 kHz that music needs, a simple player would need a very sharp analog filter; a multirate technique is the more common solution.

Sources: S. W. Smith (1997), Ch. 22 “High Fidelity Audio”; IEC 60908.

HDR television

BT.2100 codes each component with 10 or 12 bits and adds the PQ and HLG transfer functions. PQ is tuned to the human visual system so that a very wide range of brightness fits into that bit depth.

Source: ITU-R BT.2100-3.

Learning reflection

Before this section I treated resolution and bit depth as quality settings in an export dialog. Reading Shannon’s theorem next to the G.711 and BT.2100 standards showed me that each parameter answers a different question: how often we measure, and how finely we write the measurement down. Calculating the raw data rates made the cost concrete. One second of uncompressed 1080p video carries about 23,000 times the data of one second of a phone call. The idea that a pixel is a point sample, not a square, also changed how I picture enlarging an image. The part I still find hard is predicting aliasing in pictures, because it is easy to hear in audio examples but harder to see in images, so I will revisit it through the resizing page.

Key learning points

  • Sampling (where and when) and quantization (how precisely) are separate choices with separate errors: aliasing and quantization noise.
  • Raw data rate = samples per second × bits per sample × channels, so uncompressed video needs thousands of times the data of audio.
  • Extra bits help only where people can perceive the difference, which is why HDR adds bits together with perceptual transfer curves.

Misconception

“A pixel is a tiny square of colour.” A pixel is a point sample. Drawing it as a square is just one way to reconstruct the image, and usually not the best one (A. R. Smith, 1995).

Verified claim

ITU-T G.711 recommends a nominal sampling rate of 8000 samples per second and eight binary digits per sample, so one telephone channel carries 8000 × 8 = 64 kbit/s.

Checked in ITU-T G.711 (11/1988), clause 2 “Sampling rate” and clause 3.1. The 64 kbit/s figure is our multiplication of those two values.
